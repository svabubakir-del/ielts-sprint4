Taste, flavour, and the brain

Many people use the words "taste" and "flavour" interchangeably in everyday conversation, but scientific research shows they are strictly distinct phenomena. Taste is detected by the tongue's specialised receptors, which can identify only five basic qualities: sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and umami. Flavour, by contrast, is a complex neurological construction that the brain assembles from multiple sensory inputs simultaneously — primarily from gustation, retronasal olfaction, and the trigeminal nerve. When we chew food, body heat and mechanical grinding release volatile organic compounds, which travel upward through the pharynx and enter the nasal cavity from behind. The brain misattributes these smell signals to the mouth, creating the impression that flavour originates there. Chemical irritants such as capsaicin and menthol exploit the trigeminal pathway to produce the sensations we associate with spicy heat or cooling freshness. In modern food science, the process of creating synthetic flavours is highly iterative: flavourists repeatedly refine their formulations through rounds of testing until the product is deemed acceptable. Research suggests that retronasal olfaction may contribute substantially to the overall experience of flavour — some estimates place its contribution at over 80% of what we perceive.
