How vaccines train the immune system

Vaccination works by exposing the immune system to a harmless version of a pathogen's antigen, training the body to recognise and respond to it without causing the genuine disease. Edward Jenner's original discovery relied on the observation that people who had contracted the mild cowpox virus appeared resistant to the far more virulent smallpox. When the immune system encounters an antigen, specialised cells produce antibodies to neutralise it, and a subset of these cells remain dormant afterward, ready to respond far more quickly if the same pathogen is encountered again. Once enough of a population becomes immune through vaccination, a contagious disease struggles to find susceptible hosts, producing herd immunity that indirectly protects even unvaccinated individuals. This protection can be complicated when a virus mutates, altering the antigens that existing antibodies were trained to recognise, which is why some vaccines must be periodically updated to mitigate the effects of new variants. Smallpox remains the only human disease to have been fully eradicated through a sustained global vaccination campaign.

The Long Road to a Polio-Free World

Poliomyelitis, commonly known as polio, was once among the most feared contagious diseases of the twentieth century, capable of causing permanent paralysis or death, particularly among young children. The introduction of an effective vaccine in the 1950s, developed independently by Jonas Salk and later Albert Sabin using different approaches, transformed the disease from a widespread annual epidemic into a rare and increasingly geographically confined threat, offering a compelling case study in what a coordinated, sustained vaccination effort can achieve.

Unlike smallpox, however, polio has proven considerably more difficult to eradicate entirely, despite a global eradication initiative launched in 1988 that has already reduced case numbers by more than ninety-nine percent worldwide. Several factors complicate the final stages of eradication. The virus can transmit through contaminated water in regions lacking adequate sanitation infrastructure, and a significant proportion of infections produce no visible symptoms at all, allowing the pathogen to continue circulating undetected within a population even as visible case numbers decline.

Political instability and armed conflict in certain remaining endemic regions have further complicated vaccination efforts, as health workers attempting to administer vaccines have occasionally faced serious security risks, and disrupted infrastructure has made sustaining the very high vaccination coverage required to interrupt transmission entirely difficult to achieve and maintain. Even a temporary lapse in vaccination coverage within a susceptible population can allow a dormant outbreak to resurface with alarming speed.

Public health researchers studying the polio eradication campaign note that the final stages of eradicating any disease tend to be disproportionately difficult and costly compared to the earlier stages of reducing overall case numbers, since eliminating the last remaining pockets of transmission requires reaching precisely the populations that have historically proven hardest to access through conventional public health infrastructure.

Passage A — mRNA Vaccines: A New Approach

Traditional vaccines typically introduce a weakened, inactivated, or partial version of a pathogen directly into the body to trigger an immune response. Messenger RNA, or mRNA, vaccines take a fundamentally different approach: rather than delivering pathogen material directly, they deliver genetic instructions that direct the body's own cells to temporarily produce a harmless antigen fragment, which the immune system then recognises and responds to as though encountering the actual pathogen. Because mRNA vaccines do not require growing large quantities of live virus during manufacturing, a process traditionally required for many conventional vaccines, they can in principle be developed and produced considerably faster once a pathogen's genetic sequence becomes available. Once the antigen fragment has been produced and the immune response triggered, the introduced mRNA is rapidly broken down by the body's normal cellular processes, meaning it does not persist or integrate into the recipient's own genetic material.

Passage B — Cold Chains and Vaccine Distribution

Developing an effective vaccine represents only part of the challenge in achieving widespread immunization; successfully distributing that vaccine to the populations who need it presents a substantial logistical challenge in its own right. Many vaccines require storage within a narrow temperature range throughout their entire journey from manufacturing facility to the point of administration, a requirement known within public health circles as maintaining an unbroken cold chain. A single significant break in this cold chain, whether caused by equipment failure, extended power outages, or transportation delays, can render an entire shipment of vaccine doses ineffective, even when the vaccine appears physically unchanged and shows no obvious visible signs of having been compromised. This logistical requirement poses particular difficulties in remote or under-resourced regions lacking reliable electricity infrastructure, sometimes requiring specialised solar-powered refrigeration units or careful advance route planning to ensure vaccines remain within their required temperature range throughout transport and storage.

Passage 1 — Louis Pasteur and the Rabies Vaccine

Nearly a century after Edward Jenner's original smallpox work, the French scientist Louis Pasteur developed a vaccine against rabies, a virtually always fatal disease once symptoms appear, using a weakened form of the virus grown in laboratory conditions. In 1885, Pasteur controversially administered this experimental vaccine directly to a young boy who had been severely bitten by a rabid dog, despite Pasteur himself lacking formal medical qualifications to treat patients directly. The boy survived, providing a dramatic public demonstration of vaccination's potential and establishing Pasteur's broader germ theory of disease as a legitimate and increasingly influential scientific framework, considerably advancing the field of immunology beyond Jenner's earlier, more empirically-driven approach.

Passage 2 — Vaccine Hesitancy: A Public Health Challenge

Despite the well-documented effectiveness of vaccination programmes, public health researchers consistently identify vaccine hesitancy — reluctance or refusal to vaccinate despite vaccine availability — as a persistent challenge to achieving the population coverage required for herd immunity. Researchers studying this phenomenon note that hesitancy stems from a wide range of factors, including concerns about vaccine safety, distrust of public health institutions, and the influence of misinformation circulating through social networks. Public health communication strategies aimed at addressing hesitancy have shown mixed effectiveness, with researchers generally agreeing that approaches tailored to a community's specific underlying concerns tend to prove more successful than broad, generic public awareness campaigns.

Passage 3 — Vaccines for Diseases That Do Not Exist Yet

In recent years, some researchers have begun developing what are sometimes termed prototype vaccines against entire families of viruses believed to pose a significant future pandemic risk, rather than waiting for a specific novel pathogen to emerge before beginning vaccine development. This approach, sometimes called prototype pathogen preparedness, aims to substantially compress the historically lengthy vaccine development timeline by completing much of the foundational research in advance, so that a vaccine against a genuinely novel but related pathogen could, in principle, be rapidly adapted from existing research once the new threat is identified, rather than requiring development to begin entirely from scratch.

Passage 4 — Veterinary Vaccination and Zoonotic Disease

Vaccination programmes are not limited to human populations; veterinary vaccination plays a significant role in preventing zoonotic diseases, meaning diseases capable of transmitting between animals and humans. Rabies vaccination campaigns targeting domestic dog populations, for instance, have proven highly effective at reducing human rabies cases in regions where the disease was historically transmitted primarily through dog bites, since interrupting transmission within the animal reservoir population indirectly protects human populations without requiring universal human vaccination. Public health researchers increasingly advocate for a coordinated approach, sometimes termed "one health," that considers human, animal, and environmental health as interconnected when designing disease prevention strategies rather than addressing each in isolation.
