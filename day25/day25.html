How do birds find their way?

Every year, migratory birds complete journeys spanning thousands of kilometres, often navigating with astonishing precise accuracy despite many individuals having never previously travelled the route. Researchers have identified several systems that appear to work together to allow birds to orient themselves so reliably. Magnetoreception, the ability to sense the Earth's geomagnetic field, is one leading hypothesis, with some evidence pointing to a light-sensitive protein in the retina and other evidence pointing to iron-oxide crystals in the beak. Celestial cues also play an important role: daytime migrants calibrate an internal sun compass using patterns of polarized light, while nocturnal migrants rely on the rotational center of the night sky. Disruption experiments, in which one navigational cue is deliberately altered, have shown that artificial magnetic fields reliably cause disorientation in test birds, providing strong empirical support for the role of magnetoreception. Naturally occurring magnetic anomalies can also disrupt migratory behaviour, though the precise threshold at which a disturbance becomes significant remains an active area of research.

Tracking the Untrackable: Technology and the Study of Migration

For most of the twentieth century, the study of bird migration was severely constrained by a basic technological limitation: researchers had no reliable way of following an individual bird across an entire migratory journey. Early efforts relied almost entirely on bird ringing, in which a small numbered metal band is attached to a bird's leg, allowing researchers to identify an individual if it was ever recaptured or found again, often thousands of kilometres from where it was first ringed. While ringing provided valuable point-to-point data, it offered no information about the actual route taken, the speed of travel, or the specific stopover sites used along the way.

The development of miniaturized satellite transmitters in the late twentieth century transformed the field. For the first time, researchers could track individual birds continuously throughout an entire migratory journey, revealing details that had previously been entirely invisible: precise flight paths, the exact timing and duration of rest stops, and the specific environmental conditions under which birds chose to travel. This technology confirmed that many species follow remarkably consistent routes year after year, often within a few kilometres of the previous year's path, despite the individual bird facing no external guidance beyond its own internal navigational systems.

More recently, geolocators — lightweight devices that record ambient light levels to estimate a bird's approximate position — have made it possible to study even very small migratory species that cannot carry a full satellite transmitter. Because geolocators must be physically recovered from the bird to download their data, researchers face the practical challenge of recapturing an individual bird after it completes a round-trip migration spanning tens of thousands of kilometres, a task that succeeds only a fraction of the time.

Despite these technological advances, a comprehensive picture of avian navigation remains elusive. Tracking data reveals where and when birds travel with remarkable detail, but reveals comparatively little about which internal cues a bird is relying on at any given moment, or how it resolves conflicting information when multiple navigational systems disagree. Combining tracking technology with the kind of controlled disruption experiments used in laboratory settings remains one of the most promising, if logistically demanding, directions for future research.

Passage A — The Bar-Tailed Godwit's Non-Stop Flight

Among all migratory birds, few journeys demonstrate physical endurance as dramatically as the non-stop flight of the bar-tailed godwit. Tracking data has confirmed that individuals of this species can fly for more than eleven thousand kilometres across the Pacific Ocean without landing, stopping to feed, or resting even briefly, a journey that can take more than a week of continuous flight. To sustain this extraordinary effort, the godwit undergoes dramatic physiological changes before departure, including the temporary shrinking of its digestive organs to reduce body weight and the accumulation of substantial fat reserves that fuel the entire journey. Researchers consider this species one of the most compelling examples of how natural selection can shape an organism's physiology around the specific demands of an extreme migratory strategy.

Passage B — Light Pollution and Migratory Disorientation

One of the more concerning modern threats to migratory bird navigation is artificial light pollution. Nocturnal migrants, which rely heavily on celestial cues such as the pattern of stars for orientation, can become severely disoriented by the bright lights of cities and other human infrastructure encountered along their route. Birds have been observed circling brightly lit structures for extended periods, apparently unable to reorient themselves using their normal celestial compass once their view of the natural night sky has been obscured or overwhelmed by artificial light. In extreme cases, this disorientation results in fatal collisions with buildings, an outcome that some conservation groups estimate causes hundreds of millions of bird deaths annually. Several cities have introduced voluntary lights-out programmes during peak migration periods, and preliminary evidence suggests that reducing artificial light during these windows can measurably reduce collision rates.

Passage 1 — The Arctic Tern's Extraordinary Range

The Arctic tern holds the record for the longest annual migration of any known animal, travelling from its Arctic breeding grounds to Antarctic waters and back again each year, a round trip that can exceed seventy thousand kilometres. Because the tern experiences summer conditions in both hemispheres during different parts of its annual cycle, researchers estimate that an individual Arctic tern may see more daylight over its lifetime than any other creature on the planet. This extreme migratory strategy is thought to have evolved as a means of exploiting the abundant food resources available in polar waters during each hemisphere's respective summer season.

Passage 2 — Weather Radar and Migration Monitoring

In recent decades, meteorologists and ornithologists have discovered an unexpected secondary use for weather radar systems: tracking the mass movement of migratory birds. Because bird flocks reflect radar signals in a manner distinct from precipitation, trained analysts can distinguish migratory movements from weather patterns on radar imagery, allowing researchers to monitor the timing, direction, and scale of migration across entire regions in real time. This method has proven particularly valuable for identifying nights of unusually intense migratory activity, information that some cities now use to trigger voluntary lights-out advisories aimed at reducing collision risk for nocturnal migrants passing overhead.

Passage 3 — Juvenile Navigation Without Adult Guidance

Among the most striking findings in migration research is that juvenile cuckoos complete their first migratory journey entirely without adult guidance, departing weeks after their parents have already left and following an inherited route to wintering grounds they have never previously visited. Because the young birds cannot possibly have learned the route through experience or observation, researchers consider this one of the strongest pieces of evidence that at least some components of migratory navigation must be genetically inherited rather than learned. Exactly how such detailed route information could be encoded genetically remains an open and actively researched question.

Passage 4 — Climate Change and Shifting Migration Timing

Long-term monitoring data has revealed that many migratory bird species are now departing on their spring migration earlier than they did several decades ago, a shift widely attributed to changing climate conditions. This creates a potential mismatch problem: birds that rely on the seasonal timing of food availability at their breeding grounds may arrive too early or too late relative to the peak abundance of the insects or plants their offspring depend on. Species that show greater flexibility in adjusting their migratory timing in response to environmental cues appear better able to cope with these shifts, while species with more rigid, largely innate timing mechanisms face growing pressure to adapt at a pace that may outstrip their evolutionary capacity to do so.
