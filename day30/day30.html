From launch to orbit

Reaching a stable orbit requires a spacecraft to achieve sufficient horizontal velocity so that, as it falls under Earth's gravitational pull, it continuously misses the ground rather than crashing into it. This demands enormous thrust generated along a carefully calculated trajectory during launch. Because a rocket must carry its own fuel as part of its total payload, and additional fuel itself adds weight, engineers face a persistent design challenge in reducing wasted mass. Uncrewed, autonomous probes have explored hostile and distant environments, including the harsh terrain of Mars, without the added complexity of keeping a human crew alive. Crewed missions remain a genuinely pioneering pursuit, with astronauts completing extravehicular activity in the vacuum of space. Returning to Earth is equally demanding: reentry into the atmosphere generates extreme heat, requiring specialised heat-resistant materials to protect the spacecraft from a trajectory that is neither too shallow nor too steep.

The Rise of Commercial Spaceflight

For much of the twentieth century, space exploration was pursued almost exclusively by government agencies, primarily as an extension of national scientific prestige and, particularly during the Cold War era, geopolitical competition. Over the past two decades, however, an increasing share of launch activity has shifted toward private commercial companies, a transition that has substantially altered the economics, and consequently the pace, of space exploration.

Perhaps the most consequential development driving this shift has been the emergence of reusable rocket technology. Historically, the vast majority of a rocket's cost was discarded after a single use, with spent stages either burning up during reentry or falling into the ocean. Companies that have successfully developed rockets capable of controlled landing and subsequent reuse have demonstrated the potential to substantially reduce the cost of reaching orbit, a development with significant implications for the future feasibility of more ambitious exploration objectives.

This shift toward commercial spaceflight has not been without controversy. Critics have raised concerns regarding the increasing accumulation of debris in Earth orbit as satellite launches multiply, alongside broader questions about appropriate regulatory oversight of an increasingly crowded and commercially significant orbital environment. Proponents counter that competitive commercial pressure has driven genuine innovation and cost reduction at a pace historically difficult to achieve within traditional government-funded space programmes alone.

Looking ahead, many industry analysts anticipate that continued cost reductions driven by commercial competition may prove instrumental in eventually making sustained human presence beyond low Earth orbit, including potential future Mars missions, a genuinely economically viable long-term proposition rather than a purely government-subsidised scientific and exploratory endeavour.

Passage A — Life Support Systems

Sustaining human life in the hostile vacuum of space requires an intricately engineered life support system capable of continuously supplying breathable air, regulating temperature, removing carbon dioxide, and managing waste, all within a tightly sealed module offering essentially zero margin for equipment failure. Modern life support systems increasingly incorporate recycling technology, reclaiming water from sources including exhaled breath and, notably, treated urine, a capability that dramatically reduces the total mass of consumable resources that must otherwise be launched from Earth for long-duration missions. As mission durations extend toward multi-year Mars expeditions, engineers face mounting pressure to develop increasingly self-sufficient, closed-loop life support systems capable of operating reliably for years without any resupply from Earth, a considerably more demanding engineering standard than that required for current low Earth orbit missions.

Passage B — The Search for Habitable Worlds

Beyond crewed and uncrewed exploration of our own solar system, a distinct branch of space science focuses on identifying potentially habitable planets orbiting distant stars, commonly termed exoplanets. Researchers primarily search for exoplanets located within a star's so-called habitable zone, an orbital distance neither too close nor too far from the parent star for liquid water to plausibly exist on a planet's surface, a condition widely considered a baseline requirement for life as currently understood. Detecting an exoplanet directly remains extraordinarily difficult given the vast distances involved, so astronomers instead rely primarily on indirect detection methods, such as measuring the slight periodic dimming of a star's light as an orbiting planet passes directly in front of it. While thousands of exoplanets have now been confirmed using these methods, definitively determining whether any given exoplanet is genuinely habitable, let alone actually inhabited, remains far beyond the reach of current observational technology.

Passage 1 — The Space Race Origins

The launch of Sputnik 1 in 1957, the first artificial satellite to reach orbit, is widely regarded as the opening milestone of the Cold War-era space race between the United States and the Soviet Union. The launch prompted considerable public and political concern in the United States regarding perceived technological competitiveness, ultimately contributing to substantially increased government investment in science education and space-related research over the following decade. This intense period of competitive investment produced a remarkably rapid sequence of milestones, from the first crewed orbital flight to the first crewed Moon landing within little more than a decade of Sputnik's launch.

Passage 2 — Microgravity and the Human Body

Extended exposure to microgravity conditions aboard a space station produces measurable physiological changes in the human body, including gradual bone density loss and muscle atrophy, since the body's bones and muscles no longer bear weight against gravity in the way they constantly do on Earth's surface. Astronauts undertake rigorous daily exercise routines specifically designed to help mitigate these effects during long-duration missions, though current countermeasures do not entirely eliminate the physiological toll of extended spaceflight. Understanding and further mitigating these effects remains an active area of research, considered particularly critical for the substantially longer mission durations anticipated for future crewed Mars expeditions.

Passage 3 — Space Debris: An Emerging Hazard

Decades of satellite launches have left an accumulating quantity of defunct spacecraft, spent rocket stages, and fragmentary debris in orbit around Earth, a growing hazard that space agencies increasingly monitor with considerable concern. Even relatively small fragments of debris travel at extremely high orbital velocities, meaning a collision with an operational satellite or crewed spacecraft could cause serious damage despite the debris fragment's small physical size. Some researchers warn of a theoretical scenario, sometimes called the Kessler syndrome, in which cascading collisions between debris fragments could progressively generate further debris, potentially rendering certain orbital regions increasingly hazardous for future satellite operations over time.

Passage 4 — Why Return to the Moon?

Renewed interest in crewed lunar missions, decades after the conclusion of the original Apollo programme, is frequently framed by space agencies as a deliberate stepping stone toward more ambitious future Mars missions rather than as an end in itself. Proponents argue that establishing a sustained lunar presence would allow astronauts and engineers to test life support systems, resource extraction techniques, and other critical technologies under conditions considerably less hostile and more quickly reachable than a distant Mars expedition, before committing to the substantially greater risks and logistical demands of a multi-year crewed Mars mission.
