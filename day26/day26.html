From hand ciphers to public-key encryption

Cryptography has evolved dramatically since the earliest hand ciphers were used to conceal military messages. Simple substitution ciphers, in which each letter of the plaintext was systematically replaced to produce ciphertext, proved vulnerable because certain letters occur more frequently than others in any language, allowing an intercepted message to be deciphered through statistical analysis. The German Enigma machine represented a major leap in complexity, using rotating wheels to generate a different substitution for every letter typed, a system German commanders believed to be genuinely unbreakable. A classified, systematic codebreaking effort at Bletchley Park ultimately succeeded in breaking Enigma. Modern cryptography relies instead on public-key algorithms, exploiting a mathematical asymmetry between multiplying large prime numbers, which is fast, and factoring the resulting product, which is extraordinarily difficult without the correct private key. Even so, no cryptographic system is considered permanently secure; practical vulnerabilities more often arise from ambiguous code or poorly authenticated users than from the underlying mathematics itself.

The Women Codebreakers of Bletchley Park

While the mathematician Alan Turing has become the most widely recognised figure associated with the Allied codebreaking effort during the Second World War, a substantial proportion of the workforce at Bletchley Park — estimates suggest as much as three-quarters — consisted of women, many of whom performed technically demanding roles that have only recently received the historical recognition they deserve. Recruited from universities, the civil service, and the armed forces, these women worked as codebreakers, translators, and operators of the enormous electromechanical machines used to test possible Enigma settings systematically.

For decades, the wartime work of Bletchley Park remained officially classified, and many veterans, women and men alike, were legally bound by strict secrecy obligations that prevented them from discussing their contributions even with close family members. This enforced silence meant that the specific contributions of individual codebreakers, and women in particular, went largely unrecorded and unacknowledged in early historical accounts of the war, which tended to focus disproportionately on a small number of prominent male figures.

Only with the declassification of substantial archival material beginning in the 1970s, and continuing in stages over subsequent decades, did historians gain sufficient access to reconstruct a more complete and accurate picture of the codebreaking effort. This ongoing process of historical reassessment has gradually revealed the essential, and previously underappreciated, contributions of the women who operated the codebreaking machinery, developed procedural shortcuts, and in some documented cases made genuine cryptographic breakthroughs of their own.

Historians studying this period now generally agree that the popular narrative crediting codebreaking success to a small handful of individual geniuses considerably oversimplifies what was, in practice, an enormous and highly systematic collaborative effort involving thousands of people working in tightly coordinated teams.

Passage A — The One-Time Pad: A Theoretically Unbreakable Cipher

Among all known encryption methods, only one has been mathematically proven to be theoretically unbreakable when used correctly: the one-time pad. The method requires a truly random key, exactly as long as the message itself, used only once and never reused for any subsequent communication. Provided these strict conditions are met, a one-time pad produces ciphertext that offers an attacker absolutely no statistical information whatsoever about the original plaintext, rendering even unlimited computational power theoretically useless against it. Despite this remarkable theoretical security, the one-time pad has seen limited practical use, primarily because securely generating, distributing, and storing a truly random key as long as every message ever sent presents an enormous logistical challenge, particularly for large-scale communication. Historical instances of one-time pad reuse, whether through operational error or resource shortages, have in fact provided codebreakers with rare and valuable opportunities to compromise otherwise theoretically unbreakable encrypted traffic.

Passage B — Quantum Computing and the Future of Encryption

The eventual development of sufficiently powerful quantum computers poses a potentially serious threat to much of the public-key cryptography currently securing digital communication worldwide. Unlike conventional computers, which test possibilities largely one at a time, a sufficiently advanced quantum computer could, in principle, factor the large prime numbers underlying current encryption algorithms far more rapidly, potentially rendering widely deployed systems vulnerable within a practical timeframe rather than requiring longer than the age of the universe. In anticipation of this threat, cryptography researchers have already begun developing and standardising so-called post-quantum algorithms, designed specifically to remain secure even against an adversary equipped with a functioning quantum computer. Critics caution, however, that migrating the enormous existing infrastructure of digital communication to new cryptographic standards is a slow, complex, and expensive undertaking, and that organisations delaying this transition risk having historically encrypted data retroactively compromised once sufficiently powerful quantum computers eventually become available.

Passage 1 — The Caesar Cipher

One of the earliest documented ciphers is attributed to Julius Caesar, who reportedly used a simple substitution method to protect military correspondence from being understood if intercepted by an enemy. Each letter of the plaintext was shifted a fixed number of positions through the alphabet, producing a ciphertext that appeared meaningless to anyone unaware of the specific shift used. Although trivially easy to break using modern techniques, requiring only twenty-five possible shifts to test systematically, the Caesar cipher is historically significant as one of the first documented examples of using a formal, repeatable algorithm to protect written communication.

Passage 2 — The Navajo Code Talkers

During the Second World War, the United States military recruited Navajo speakers to develop and transmit coded messages based on their native language, an approach that proved remarkably effective largely because Navajo was an unwritten language with an extremely complex grammatical structure unfamiliar to Japanese cryptanalysts. Unlike a mathematical cipher, the Navajo code relied on a specialised vocabulary of substitute terms for military concepts, memorised by trained code talkers rather than encoded through any systematic algorithm. Japanese codebreakers never succeeded in deciphering the Navajo-based code throughout the entire war, making it one of the few wartime codes never confirmed to have been broken by an adversary.

Passage 3 — Steganography: Hiding the Message Itself

Distinct from cryptography, which conceals the meaning of a message while leaving its existence obvious, steganography instead conceals the very existence of a hidden message within an apparently innocent carrier, such as an image, an audio file, or ordinary text. A classic historical example involved concealing a message within the pattern of stitches in a piece of needlework, while modern digital steganography can hide encrypted data within the least significant bits of a digital photograph, altering the image in a way imperceptible to the human eye. Security researchers note that cryptography and steganography are frequently combined, since concealing the existence of an encrypted message provides an additional layer of protection beyond encryption alone.

Passage 4 — Password Hashing and Everyday Cryptography

Most people encounter practical applications of cryptography daily without necessarily recognising it as such, particularly through the process of password hashing used by nearly every secure online service. Rather than storing a user's password directly, which would create a serious vulnerability if a database were ever compromised, a website instead stores the output of a one-way mathematical function called a hash, applied to the original password. Because a properly designed hash function is computationally impractical to reverse, an attacker who obtains a database of hashed passwords cannot straightforwardly recover the original plaintext passwords, although weak or commonly used passwords remain vulnerable to systematic guessing attacks that compare hashed guesses against the stolen data.
